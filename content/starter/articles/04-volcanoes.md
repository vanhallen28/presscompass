Indonesia has about 500 volcanoes, and the Geological Agency (Badan Geologi) counts 127 of them as active. They run in a long arc from Sumatra through Java, Bali and Nusa Tenggara, and up through Sulawesi and Maluku, where the Indo-Australian plate slides beneath the Eurasian plate.

## Who keeps watch

The work falls to the Centre for Volcanology and Geological Hazard Mitigation, known by its Indonesian initials PVMBG, part of the Geological Agency in the Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources. Its staff work from 74 observation posts, usually a few kilometres from a crater, and monitor 69 active volcanoes in real time.

They track earthquakes beneath each mountain, the swelling or sinking of its slopes, the gases it gives off and what can be seen of the summit. The readings and daily reports are published through MAGMA Indonesia, the agency's public website and app, so local governments and residents can see the same information as the scientists.

## The four alert levels

Each active volcano is given one of four alert levels:

- **Level I, Normal:** activity is at its usual background level.
- **Level II, Waspada (Advisory):** readings have risen above normal. People are told to stay out of a zone around the crater.
- **Level III, Siaga (Watch):** an eruption is likely or under way. The exclusion zone grows and local governments prepare to move people.
- **Level IV, Awas (Warning):** a major eruption is expected or happening. Everyone in the danger zone must be evacuated.

The exclusion zone is set for each volcano and can be shaped to follow the valleys where hot ash flows and volcanic mudflows, known as lahars, are most likely to travel.

## Where things stood

In December 2025, the head of the PVMBG, Priatin Hadi Wijaya, said three volcanoes were at Level III: Merapi on the border of Central Java and Yogyakarta, Semeru in East Java, and Lewotobi Laki-laki on Flores in East Nusa Tenggara. Another 24 were at Level II. About 15 million people lived in areas around those volcanoes. Alert levels change often, so the current status of any volcano is best checked on MAGMA Indonesia.

## Why it matters

Indonesia has been the setting for some of the deadliest eruptions on record. Tambora, on Sumbawa, erupted in 1815 in the largest eruption in recorded history, and the ash and gases it sent into the atmosphere cooled the climate around the world the following year. Krakatau, in the Sunda Strait, exploded in 1883; the tsunamis it caused killed more than 36,000 people.

Most eruptions today are much smaller, and warnings save lives. The hardest part is often not the science but the decision that follows: asking farmers to leave homes, fields and livestock for weeks or months on the strength of a rising reading.
