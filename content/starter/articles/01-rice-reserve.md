Rice is the food most Indonesian households buy every week, so its price is one of the most closely watched numbers in the country. When rice becomes more expensive, poorer families feel it first. To soften those swings, the government keeps a stockpile of its own.

## What the reserve is

The stockpile is called the government rice reserve, *cadangan beras pemerintah*, or CBP. It is held in warehouses run by Perum Bulog, the state logistics company. Policy on how much to hold and when to release it is set by the National Food Agency (Badan Pangan Nasional, or Bapanas), while Bulog does the buying, storing and selling.

The reserve has grown quickly. In April 2026, Deputy Agriculture Minister Sudaryono put it at about 4.8 million tonnes, which he called the highest in history. He spoke during a visit with President Prabowo Subianto to a Bulog warehouse complex in Magelang, Central Java.

## Where the rice comes from

Most of the reserve is now bought at home. The government has told Bulog to absorb at least 4 million tonnes of domestic rice in 2026, up from 3 million tonnes the year before, and allocated Rp16.5 trillion in 2025 to support that role. Buying during the harvest also helps farmers: when markets are flooded with newly harvested grain, a large buyer at a set price keeps farm-gate prices from collapsing.

The ministry says rice production rose 13.29 per cent in 2025, which it attributes to more planted area and government programmes. Counting the reserve, rice already in circulation and the coming harvests, it projects total supply of about 28 million tonnes, or 11 months of consumption.

## How it reaches the market

The reserve is not meant to sit in warehouses. Its main outlet is a programme called SPHP, short for *Stabilisasi Pasokan dan Harga Pangan* (food supply and price stabilisation). Under SPHP, Bulog sells reserve rice into traditional markets and shops at a controlled price when retail prices climb, adding supply where it is short. Reserve rice is also used for food aid to low-income households and for emergencies such as floods or earthquakes.

## The trade-offs

A large stockpile is not free. Rice must be dried, stored and turned over, and grain kept too long loses quality. The National Food Agency has asked Bulog to look after the quality of the reserve, because rice that sits for many months can become unsuitable for sale.

There is also a balance to strike between farmers and consumers. Buying more from farmers supports their income; releasing more into markets keeps prices down for city households. Officials describe their pricing policy as protecting both. How well that balance holds is the question to watch in each harvest and each lean season.
