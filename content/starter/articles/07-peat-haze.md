Indonesia is home to about half of the world's tropical peatland, most of it in Sumatra, Kalimantan and Papua. Peat forms over thousands of years from plants that fall into waterlogged ground and never fully rot. In its natural state it is about 90 per cent water, and it rarely burns.

## Why drained peat burns

The trouble starts when peat is drained, usually by digging canals to prepare land for plantations or farming. As the water table drops, the top layers dry into a mass of dead leaves, roots and wood that catches fire easily. Once alight, peat can smoulder underground for weeks, out of reach of water bombers and hard to find from the air. According to the UN-REDD Programme, burning peat can release ten times more carbon than a forest fire, and it produces the thick, low smoke known across the region as haze.

Most fires are started by people, often to clear land cheaply. Dry seasons made longer by El Niño, the periodic warming of the Pacific, turn those fires into disasters.

## 2015 and after

In 2015, an El Niño year, about 2.6 million hectares burned. The World Bank put the cost to Indonesia's economy at US$16.1 billion. In response, the government created the Peatland Restoration Agency (BRG) in January 2016 with a target of restoring 2.4 million hectares of damaged peat. Rules adopted the same year require the water table in peat used for plantations to be kept within 40 centimetres of the surface.

Restoration relied heavily on blocking canals to keep water in the peat, and on training villages to build and maintain the blocks. The agency was later renamed the Peatland and Mangrove Restoration Agency (BRGM), and its temporary mandate ended in 2024. Fires returned on a smaller scale in 2019, when about 1.65 million hectares were affected.

## The 2026 season

This year has brought the worst fires in a decade, with a strong El Niño forecast to last into early 2027.

South Papua has been the worst-affected province. Smoke has closed schools in Sarawak in Malaysia and lowered air quality in Singapore and southern Thailand. In a July study, the monitoring group Pantau Gambut found that 70 per cent of the canal blocks it inspected were damaged, and that groundwater at more than half of the restoration sites it visited was below the safe level.

The government has revoked the permits of five companies and frozen dozens more, and in September President Prabowo Subianto ordered a new agency to manage peat and mangrove ecosystems and prevent fires.

## What would help

Researchers broadly agree on the basics: keep peat wet all year rather than only in the dry season, repair and maintain canal blocks, enforce the rules on companies whose land burns, and give villagers ways to clear and farm land without fire. Indonesia ratified the ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution in 2014, but the agreement depends on national enforcement. Whether this year's measures last beyond the rains will decide how the next El Niño goes.
