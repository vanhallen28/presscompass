On the morning of 17 August 1945, two days after Japan announced its surrender in the Second World War, Sukarno read a short proclamation of independence outside his house at Jalan Pegangsaan Timur 56 in Jakarta, with Mohammad Hatta at his side. They became the first president and vice-president of the Republic of Indonesia. The Netherlands, the former colonial power, recognised Indonesian sovereignty at the end of 1949, after four years of war and negotiation.

## The ceremony

Every year on 17 August, the president leads a flag-raising ceremony in front of Merdeka Palace in Jakarta. A team of selected high-school students, the Paskibraka, raises the red-and-white flag at the moment that marks the proclamation, and the text of the proclamation is read aloud. In the afternoon, the flag is lowered in a second ceremony.

In 2024, the main ceremony was held for the first time in Nusantara, the new capital being built in East Kalimantan. For the 81st anniversary in 2026, it took place at Merdeka Palace, with President Prabowo Subianto as inspector of the ceremony and at least 8,100 members of the public given places through a ticket lottery. At 10.17 Western Indonesia Time, people across the country were asked to stop what they were doing and stand while the flag was raised.

## A month of events

The government calls August the month of independence. The official programme for 2026, under the theme "Indonesia Berdaulat, Adil, dan Makmur" (a sovereign, just and prosperous Indonesia), included:

- prayers for the nation at the National Monument in Jakarta on 1 August;
- the president's state address to the annual session of the People's Consultative Assembly and the joint session of the DPR and DPD on 14 August, followed by the presentation of the 2027 state budget bill;
- a night of reflection at the Kalibata Heroes' Cemetery in South Jakarta on 15 August;
- the flag-raising and flag-lowering ceremonies on 17 August.

## In the neighbourhoods

Away from the palace, the anniversary belongs to neighbourhoods and villages. Streets are decorated with red-and-white flags and bunting, and residents organise games known as *lomba*: sack races, eating crackers hanging from a string with hands behind the back, and *panjat pinang*, in which teams climb a greased pole to reach prizes at the top. For many Indonesians, these games are the part of the day they remember best.
